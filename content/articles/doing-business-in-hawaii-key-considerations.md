# Doing Business in Hawaiʻi: Key Considerations

Hawaiʻi offers a distinct — and distinctly different — business environment compared to the mainland United States. Whether you're launching a new venture, relocating an existing business, or expanding an out-of-state company into the islands, there are several state-specific rules and realities worth understanding up front.

## 1. Choose the Right Business Structure

Hawaiʻi recognizes the standard range of business entities: sole proprietorships, general partnerships, limited partnerships (LPs), limited liability partnerships (LLPs), limited liability companies (LLCs), and corporations (profit and nonprofit). An LLC is a common option for small businesses because it can provide liability protection with comparatively straightforward compliance requirements, while a corporation may suit businesses seeking outside investment.

Hawaiʻi generally requires business entities, including general partnerships, to make the applicable filings with the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA), Business Registration Division (BREG). Sole proprietors do not file entity-formation documents, although they may register a trade name and may have other licensing and tax obligations. Hawaiʻi does not have a separate Secretary of State office; BREG performs that function.

## 2. Registering Your Business

Domestic entities register by filing formation documents with BREG:

- **LLCs** file Articles of Organization (Form LLC-1) — $50 + $1 state archives fee
- **Profit corporations** file Articles of Incorporation (Form DC-1) — $50 + $1 State Archives fee
- **Nonprofit corporations** file Articles of Incorporation (Form DNP-1) — $25 + $1 State Archives fee

Filings can be submitted online through Hawaiʻi Business Express, or by mail, email, fax, or in person. LLCs and corporations must maintain a registered agent with the Hawaiʻi address required for their entity type to receive legal notices. Check BREG's requirements for other entity structures.

## 3. If You're Coming From Out of State: Foreign Qualification

An out-of-state entity may need to register as a **foreign entity** by filing an Application for Certificate of Authority with BREG if its activities constitute transacting business in Hawaiʻi. Having employees or an ongoing local operation can warrant review, but sales to Hawaiʻi customers alone do not necessarily establish a foreign-registration obligation. The applicable rules depend on entity type and activities; tax-registration requirements must be evaluated separately. Requirements for entities that must qualify generally include:

- A Certificate of Good Standing (called a Certificate of Existence in some states) from your home jurisdiction, dated within the last 60 days
- A Hawaiʻi registered agent
- The applicable filing fee (comparable to domestic formation fees)

Hawaiʻi law doesn't precisely define "transacting business," so if you're unsure whether your activity crosses that line, it's worth consulting a Hawaiʻi attorney — operating without required qualification can limit your ability to bring a lawsuit in Hawaiʻi courts until you register.

## 4. Understand the General Excise Tax (GET)

Hawaiʻi has no traditional sales tax. Instead, nearly all business activity — retail sales, services, rentals, contracting, and more — is subject to the **General Excise Tax**, a tax on gross business income rather than profit. The standard GET rate for many retail and service activities is 4%, plus a 0.5% surcharge currently imposed by all four counties, for a combined 4.5%. Other activities may have different rates. Businesses may choose whether to pass GET on to customers; for applicable 4.5%-rate transactions, the maximum visible pass-on rate is 4.7120%.

To operate legally, you must register for a GET license (Form BB-1, $20 fee) with the Department of Taxation before conducting business, and then file periodic and annual GET returns going forward. Because GET applies to gross receipts, it's owed even during slow periods or years without a profit — factor it into your pricing and cash flow planning from the start.

## 5. Employer Obligations

If you plan to hire employees in Hawaiʻi, several state-specific coverages apply in addition to standard federal requirements:

- **Workers' Compensation Insurance** is required for most Hawaiʻi employers with one or more employees, subject to statutory exclusions. Penalties for failing to obtain required coverage can be at least $500 or $100 per employee for each day without coverage.
- **Temporary Disability Insurance (TDI)** is a Hawaiʻi-specific requirement — employers generally must provide wage-replacement coverage for eligible employees who become disabled from a non-work-related injury or illness. Employers may pay the entire premium or share the cost with eligible employees within statutory contribution limits.
- **Unemployment Insurance** contributions are required through the Hawaiʻi Department of Labor and Industrial Relations.
- **Prepaid Health Care (PHC)** coverage is generally required for eligible employees who work at least 20 hours per week, meet the applicable earnings threshold, and complete four consecutive weeks of employment, subject to statutory exclusions and permitted waivers.

These obligations exist on top of, not instead of, federal employment requirements like an EIN, I-9 verification, and payroll tax withholding.

## 6. Ongoing Compliance: Annual Reports

After the registration year, LLCs, corporations, LPs, and LLPs generally must file an **Annual Report** with BREG to remain in good standing. General partnerships file annual statements. An entity is not required to file an annual report or statement for the calendar year in which it registers. Unlike many states, Hawaiʻi ties the due date to the calendar quarter of your original registration date rather than a single fixed date each year:

| Registered Between | Annual Report Due |
|---|---|
| January 1 – March 31 | March 31 |
| April 1 – June 30 | June 30 |
| July 1 – September 30 | September 30 |
| October 1 – December 31 | December 31 |

Missing this filing can eventually cost your business its good standing and, after continued delinquency, lead to administrative dissolution or revocation of authority.

## 7. Licensing Beyond State Registration

Registering your entity with BREG and the Department of Taxation doesn't automatically clear every regulatory hurdle. Many professions and industries — contracting, food service, healthcare, cosmetology, real estate, liquor sales, and others — require separate state professional licenses or county permits. Some counties also have their own zoning, land use, and permitting requirements, particularly relevant for physical storefronts, agricultural operations, or short-term rentals. Check with the relevant state licensing board and your county planning department for anything specific to your industry and location.

## 8. Consider Hawaiʻi's Unique Economic and Geographic Factors

A few practical realities distinguish doing business in Hawaiʻi from doing business on the mainland:

- **Shipping and logistics costs** tend to run higher due to the state's island geography and reliance on ocean freight for most goods.
- **Commercial real estate and labor costs** can be significant operating expenses, particularly on Oʻahu; compare local quotes and compensation requirements before committing.
- **Tourism, agriculture, and military-related spending** are major pillars of the state economy, and many local business ecosystems are built around serving these sectors.
- **County-by-county differences** matter — regulations, market conditions, and GET sourcing and reporting requirements can differ depending on where business activity occurs on Oʻahu, Maui, Hawaiʻi Island, and Kaua'i.

## 9. Watch Out for Unofficial Solicitations

The DCCA has repeatedly warned businesses about third-party companies that send official-looking notices offering to file annual reports or other paperwork on a business's behalf for a fee. BREG does not authorize or use outside vendors for this purpose — verify any notice you receive by contacting BREG directly before paying or responding.

## Getting Started

Bringing these pieces together, a typical path looks like: choose your entity type → register with BREG (or file for foreign qualification) → obtain a GET license from the Department of Taxation → secure any industry-specific licenses or permits → set up required employee coverages if hiring → track your annual report deadline going forward.

For registration and licensing questions, contact BREG at (808) 586-2727. For GET registration and filing, contact the Department of Taxation. For employer coverage requirements, contact the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations' Disability Compensation Division.
